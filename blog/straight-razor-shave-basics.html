Straight-Razor Shaving: Safety, Prep & Technique Basics

By David Ayeoribe · Lead Senior Instructor & Director · November 4, 2026

The hot-towel straight-razor shave is the signature service of the classic barbershop, and one of the clearest signs of a trained professional. It is also the service where skill and safety matter most, because you are working an open blade across a client's face and neck. A great shave comes down to a handful of fundamentals: thorough skin prep, the right razor, a taut surface, a shallow angle, the right direction of travel and spotless sanitation. This guide explains each one, and why this is a service you learn under supervision, not from a video.

Why it matters

Why the straight-razor shave still matters

Clients can shave at home with a cartridge razor. What they can't easily give themselves is the full ritual: hot towels, rich lather, a close, comfortable shave and a calm half hour in the chair.

That is why the straight-razor shave remains a premium service. It sets a shop apart, gives clients a reason to book something special for a wedding, an interview or a birthday, and shows a level of control that carries over into the rest of a barber's work. The same razor skills produce the crisp lineups, edge-ups and finished beard lines clients expect on everyday cuts.

"A razor in trained hands feels effortless to the client. Everything that makes it feel that way happens before the blade ever touches skin."

At American Barber Institute, the traditional wet shave, hot-towel prep and facial massage are part of the hands-on curriculum in the 500-hour Master Barber program, alongside straight-razor lineups and detailing.

Step one

Skin prep: hot towel and lather

Most of a comfortable shave is preparation. Softened hair cuts cleanly with less pull; dry, stiff hair drags against the blade and irritates the skin.

Consult and inspect first. Before anything else, ask the client about skin sensitivity, recent irritation or breakouts, and how his skin usually reacts to shaving. Look over the face and neck. If there are open cuts, active rashes, or any condition you are unsure about, don't shave over it. Skipping or adjusting the service is part of professional judgment.

Hot towel. A warm, damp towel wrapped around the beard area softens the hair and relaxes the skin. Always test the temperature on your own wrist or forearm before it touches the client; a towel that is too hot can be uncomfortable or worse. Many barbers apply a pre-shave oil or cream before or after the towel for extra glide.

Lather. Build a rich, warm lather with a brush and work it into the beard in small circles. The brush lifts the hairs away from the skin and pushes moisture into them, which is exactly what you want before the blade. A good lather also lets you see where you have and haven't shaved. Some barbers apply a second towel and lather for very coarse beards.

The tool

Shavette vs. straight razor

Two main types of open razor show up in barbershops. Both are held and used the same basic way, but they differ in maintenance and sanitation.

Shop standard

The shavette (disposable-blade razor)

A shavette looks like a classic straight razor but holds a replaceable single-edge blade. You load a fresh blade for each client and dispose of it afterward, which makes sanitation simple and consistent. That is why disposable-blade razors are the everyday tool in most shops, and why our beginner tool kit guide lists a razor with disposable blades as a must-have.

Traditional

The classic straight razor

A true straight razor has a fixed, reusable steel blade that must be honed and stropped to keep its edge. Many barbers love the feel and the tradition. The trade-off is maintenance and sanitation: a fixed blade has to be cleaned and disinfected properly between clients, following the rules where you work.

Either way

Grip and control

Barbers learn a few standard grips, holding the shank and tang with the fingers and thumb for stability. Whatever grip you use, the motion comes from a steady, controlled hand, never a fast or forced stroke.

Technique

Stretch, angle, grain and passes

These four fundamentals are what separate a close, comfortable shave from nicks and razor burn.

Stretch the skin. A razor glides cleanly over a flat, taut surface and catches on loose skin. Use your free hand to gently pull the skin tight in the area you are shaving, and keep it dry enough to grip. The face has curves, hollows and folds, especially along the jawline, chin, upper lip and neck, so the stretch changes constantly. Learning where and how to stretch each area is a big part of training.

Keep the angle shallow. The blade should sit at a low angle to the skin, close to flat, rather than standing up. Too steep, and the edge scrapes or cuts; too flat, and it slides over the hair without cutting it. Let the sharpness of the blade do the work. Pressing harder is never the answer.

Read the grain. Hair grows in different directions on different parts of the face and neck, and the neck in particular often has swirls that change direction. Before you start, run a hand over the beard to feel which way it grows. Shaving with the grain is the gentlest; shaving against it is closest but most likely to irritate.

Work in passes. A professional shave usually builds in stages. The first pass goes with the grain to reduce the bulk comfortably. If the client wants a closer finish and his skin is handling it well, the area is re-lathered and a second pass goes across the grain. Some barbers go against the grain only on clients whose skin tolerates it. Short, controlled strokes, with the blade rinsed or wiped clean regularly, keep you in control.

Watch the tricky spots. The upper lip, under the nose, the chin, the jaw corners and the Adam's apple all need extra care, slower strokes and careful stretching. When in doubt, take a lighter pass.

The finish

Aftercare and the finishing ritual

How you finish a shave determines how the client's skin feels the next day, and whether he comes back for another one.

Rinse away any remaining lather. A cool towel helps calm the skin after the heat of the prep. Follow with a soothing aftershave balm or moisturizer appropriate for the client's skin; many clients with sensitive skin prefer alcohol-free products. Many barbers finish with a short facial massage, which turns a grooming service into a genuinely relaxing one.

Before the client leaves, give simple home-care tips: avoid harsh products on freshly shaved skin, moisturize, and keep his hands off the area. If a client mentions irritation that lasts or worries him, suggest he check with a medical professional. Barbers shouldn't diagnose skin conditions.

Non-negotiable

Sanitation, blade disposal and infection control

An open razor can break the skin, which makes sanitation the most important part of any shave. In New York, it's also part of the standard barbers are tested and licensed on.

Fresh blade

One client, one blade

With a shavette, load a new blade for every client, in front of the client if you can. It shows care and builds trust.

Disposal

Sharps go in a sharps container

Used blades go straight into a proper puncture-resistant sharps container, never loose in the trash where they can injure someone.

Clean station

Disinfect between every client

Clean and disinfect the razor handle, brushes, bowls and work surfaces, use fresh towels and neck strips, and wash your hands. Many barbers wear gloves for shaves.

This is exactly why licensing exists. New York requires barbers to learn sanitation and sterilization as part of their training, and every barber operator and apprentice application, and every renewal, must also include a short course on the Transmission of Contagious Diseases. ABI offers that course as a 3-hour home study you complete by mail; see the contagious diseases program for details. Our NY barber licensing checklist shows where it fits among the other steps.

How to learn it

Why you learn razor work under supervision

Straight-razor shaving is not a skill to practice on friends after watching a few clips. The margin for error is small, and good habits have to be built from the first stroke.

In barber school, razor work is built step by step. Students learn grips, angles and stretching, then move to supervised work on real clients, where an instructor can correct the angle of a blade or the stretch of a hand in the moment. At ABI, students train on real clinic clients under instructor supervision, so by graduation a hot-towel shave becomes muscle memory rather than theory. The same school also teaches the sanitation, sterilization and barber law that New York tests on the State Board exam. Read our guide to passing the NY barber State Board exam for more on what's covered.

Once you have the shave down, the same control powers your lineups and beard lines. See our companion piece on beard trimming and shaping.

Straight answers

Straight-razor shaving FAQ

Common questions from people curious about learning the classic shave.

What is the difference between a shavette and a straight razor?

A shavette holds a replaceable single-edge blade that you change for every client and then dispose of, which keeps sanitation simple. A traditional straight razor has a fixed steel blade that needs honing, stropping and proper cleaning and disinfection between clients. Most shops use disposable-blade razors day to day.

Why do barbers use a hot towel before a shave?

A warm towel softens the beard hair and relaxes the skin, so the blade cuts cleanly with less pulling and irritation. It also makes the service feel premium. Barbers test the towel's temperature on their own skin first so it's comfortable for the client.

Should you shave with or against the grain?

A professional shave usually starts with the grain, which is gentlest on the skin. For a closer finish, the area can be re-lathered and shaved across the grain, and against the grain only on clients whose skin tolerates it. Reading the grain carefully, especially on the neck, prevents most irritation.

Do you learn straight-razor shaving in barber school?

Yes. At American Barber Institute, the traditional wet shave, hot-towel prep and facial massage are part of the 500-hour Master Barber program, along with straight-razor lineups and detailing. Students practice on real clinic clients under instructor supervision.

How do barbers dispose of used razor blades?

Used blades go directly into a puncture-resistant sharps container, never loose in the trash. With a shavette, a fresh blade is loaded for every client, and the razor handle and the rest of the station are cleaned and disinfected between clients.

Is the contagious diseases course required for a New York barber license?

Yes. New York requires a course on the Transmission of Contagious Diseases for every barber operator and apprentice license application and for every renewal. It's separate from your training hours. ABI offers it as a 3-hour home-study course completed entirely by mail.
