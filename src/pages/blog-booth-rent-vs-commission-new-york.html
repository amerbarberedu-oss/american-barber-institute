Booth Rent vs. Commission: How New York Barbers Get Paid

Once you're licensed, the first business decision you'll make isn't about clippers or a shop's vibe. It's about how you get paid. Most New York barbers work under one of four setups: commission, where you split each ticket with the shop; booth or chair rent, where you pay the shop a fixed fee and keep what you earn; hourly plus commission, a blend that some shops use to give newer barbers a floor; and ownership, where you run the room. Each one changes who sets your prices, who controls your schedule, who buys the products, and how your taxes work. Here's how to compare them honestly and choose the right one for where you are right now.

Your pay model is a business decision, not a detail

Two barbers with the same skill, the same hours and the same clients can take home very different money depending on how their chair is structured. The pay model decides how much of every ticket you keep, how much risk you carry on a slow week, and how much freedom you have to build your own brand.

It also decides what kind of worker you are in the eyes of the shop and the tax system. A commission barber is usually closer to an employee. A booth renter is usually running a small business of one. An owner is running a business with payroll, a lease and other people's livelihoods attached. None of these is automatically "better." The right setup depends on how full your book is, how much cash cushion you have, and how much of the business side you want to handle yourself.

"Commission buys you a safety net while you build a book. Booth rent rewards you once the book is built."

Below, we break down each model in plain terms, compare them side by side, walk through the tax basics you should know (without pretending to be your accountant), and finish with a practical way to choose your first setup.

The four setups

How each pay model actually works

Every shop has its own terms, so always read the agreement. But the basic shape of each model is consistent across New York.

Model 1

Commission: you split every ticket

The shop sets the price, the client pays the shop, and you receive an agreed percentage of each service, often in the 50–60% range. In exchange, the shop usually supplies the chair, the products, the booking system, the walk-in traffic and the marketing. You typically work hours the shop schedules. Your risk is low: if the day is slow, you earn less, but you don't owe anyone money for showing up.

Model 2

Booth or chair rent: you pay a fixed fee

You pay the shop a set rent, usually weekly or monthly, and keep what you charge. In most arrangements you set your own prices, manage your own appointments and supply your own tools and products. The trade-off is that the rent is due whether your chair is full or empty. On a busy week, booth rent can put far more in your pocket. On a slow week, it can cost you.

Model 3

Hourly plus commission: a floor with upside

Some shops pay a base hourly wage and add a commission on services or retail sales on top. It's less common in independent barbershops than in larger salons and chains, but it exists. The appeal is a predictable minimum while you're still slow. The catch is that the commission portion is often smaller, and the shop controls your schedule, pricing and products just as it would under straight commission.

Model 4

Ownership: you run the room

As an owner you set the rules: pricing, hours, brand, and which pay model your barbers work under. You also carry the lease, utilities, insurance, equipment, inventory and payroll. In return, you can earn from your own chair, a share of other barbers' services or their booth rent, and retail margin. A New York Master Barber license is what qualifies you to own a shop and employ barbers and apprentices.

The switch point

When does booth rent beat commission?

The math is simpler than it looks. Booth rent starts to pay when the shop's share of your commission earnings on a full week is more than the rent you'd pay for the same period.

Here's how to run it for yourself. Track your actual weekly service revenue for a couple of months: not your best week, your typical one. Work out what the shop is keeping under your commission split. Then compare that to the booth rent at shops you'd realistically move to, and add in what you'd now be paying for yourself: products, tools, booking software, and any card processing fees. If the shop's cut is consistently bigger than all of that combined, renting is probably worth it. If it's close, stay put and keep building.

Don't forget the slow weeks. January, holiday closures, a week you're sick, a week you take off with family. On commission, a slow week just means a smaller check. On booth rent, the rent comes out of savings. Before you switch, it's wise to have a cash cushion that covers several weeks of rent with no income at all.

"Run the numbers on your typical week, not your best one. Booth rent rewards consistency, not a lucky Saturday."

Taxes, in general terms

What changes on the tax side

This is general information, not tax advice. Rules change and every situation is different, so talk to a tax professional before you choose a setup and again before your first tax season.

Employee-style

Commission and hourly

If the shop treats you as an employee, it generally withholds income and payroll taxes from your pay and you receive a W-2. That keeps things simple. Be aware that some shops pay commission barbers as independent contractors instead, which shifts the tax work onto you. Ask how you'll be paid and what form you'll receive before you start.

Self-employed

Booth rent

Booth renters are usually self-employed. That generally means no one withholds taxes for you, you may owe self-employment tax on top of income tax, and you may need to make estimated tax payments during the year. On the other side, legitimate business expenses like your booth rent, tools and products may be deductible. Keep receipts and separate your business money from your personal money from day one.

Business owner

Ownership

Owning a shop adds business registration, payroll obligations if you have employees, sales tax on retail products, insurance and more. The choice of business structure has real tax consequences. This is the stage where an accountant and, often, a lawyer stop being optional and start paying for themselves.

A simple habit that helps under any self-employed setup: every time you get paid, move a portion into a separate account set aside for taxes. Your tax professional can help you decide how much.

Pros and cons by career stage

Which model fits where you are

A common path is commission first, booth rent once your book is full, then ownership for those who want it. But the right timing is personal.

Newly licensed

Commission or hourly plus commission

Pros: steady walk-in traffic, products and marketing handled for you, experienced barbers to learn from, and no rent hanging over a slow week. Cons: you keep a smaller share of each ticket, and you have less say over pricing and hours. For most new barbers, the trade is worth it. The goal of this stage is speed, consistency and regulars.

Established

Booth rent

Pros: you keep what you charge, set your own prices and build your own brand. This is the point where many barbers see their income jump. Cons: fixed rent, your own supplies, self-employment taxes and bookkeeping, and no one filling your chair for you. It works best once clients book you by name and rebook on a regular cycle.

Experienced

Ownership

Pros: income from every chair, not just your own hands, plus full creative and business control. Cons: real start-up costs, a lease, payroll and management work. Expect it to take at least a year, often longer, to recoup the initial investment. Read whether barbershops are profitable before you sign a lease.

Your first chair

How to choose your first setup

Before you accept any chair, ask these questions. A good shop will answer them clearly.

  1. How am I paid, and how often? Get the split, the rent or the hourly rate in writing, along with whether you'll be treated as an employee or a contractor.
  2. Who supplies what? Products, towels, capes, disinfectant, booking software, card processing. Each item you supply yourself is a cost you need to plan for.
  3. Who sets prices and hours? Find out whether you can raise your prices as your skill grows and whether you choose your days.
  4. Where do new clients come from? Walk-ins, the shop's online booking, or entirely your own following? A new barber needs a shop with traffic.
  5. Who owns the client list? If you leave, can you take your book with you? Ask now, not later.
  6. What's the culture? Will senior barbers help you get faster? A shop where you can learn is worth a lot in your first year.

There's no wrong first step as long as you go in with clear terms and a plan to grow. For more on the first months after you finish school, read what happens after barber school, and for realistic income ranges at each stage, see how much barbers make in New York.

Straight answers

Booth rent vs. commission FAQ

The pay-model questions new barbers ask most, answered plainly.

Is it better to rent a booth or work on commission?

It depends on how full your book is. Commission suits barbers still building a clientele because it's low risk: you only pay the shop when you earn. Booth rent suits established barbers with a steady, full schedule who want to keep what they charge. Many barbers start on commission and switch to booth rent once their book is consistently full.

What is a typical commission split for barbers?

Splits vary by shop, but commission barbers often keep somewhere in the 50–60% range of each service, with the shop keeping the rest in exchange for the chair, products, marketing and walk-in traffic. Always get the exact terms in writing before you start.

Do booth-rent barbers pay their own taxes?

Generally, yes. Booth renters are usually self-employed, which typically means no tax withholding, possible self-employment tax, and estimated payments during the year. Business expenses may be deductible. Because the rules are detailed and change, it's best to work with a tax professional from the start.

Should a new barber start with booth rent?

Usually not. Without a steady client base, rent can eat most of what you earn on a slow week. Most new barbers do better starting on commission or hourly plus commission, where the shop brings in traffic and covers products, and moving to booth rent once clients book them by name.

What license do I need to own a barbershop in New York?

A New York Master Barber license qualifies you to own a shop and employ barbers and apprentices. You earn it by completing 500 hours of approved training and passing the New York State written and practical exams. Check current requirements for operating a shop with the New York Department of State before you open.

Can I switch from commission to booth rent later?

Yes, and that's the most common path. The switch usually makes sense when the shop's share of your commission earnings on a typical week is more than the rent plus the supplies you'd now cover yourself. Build a cash cushion first so a slow week doesn't put you behind on rent.
