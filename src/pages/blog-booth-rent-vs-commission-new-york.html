Is Booth Rent or Commission Better for New York Barbers?

For most new barbers, commission is better at first: the shop supplies the clients, products and chair, and you never owe rent on a slow week. Booth rent pays more once your book is full, because you keep what you charge after a fixed fee. The switch makes sense when the shop's cut of a typical week is bigger than the rent plus the supplies you would cover yourself.

The four setups

How does each barber pay model work?

Every shop writes its own terms, but the basic shape of each model is the same across New York.

Model 1

Commission: you split every ticket

The shop sets the price and pays you an agreed share of each service, often in the 50–60% range. In return it usually covers the chair, products, booking, walk-ins and marketing, and sets your hours. A slow day means a smaller check, never a bill.

Model 2

Booth rent: you pay a fixed fee

You pay a set weekly or monthly rent and keep what you charge. You usually set your prices, run your own bookings and buy your own products. The rent is due whether your chair is full or empty, so a busy week pays well and a slow one costs you.

Model 3

Hourly plus commission: a floor with upside

Some shops, more often chains than independent barbershops, pay a base hourly wage plus commission. You get a predictable minimum while you are slow, but a smaller commission share, and the shop controls pricing and schedule.

Model 4

Ownership: you run the room

As an owner you set prices, hours, brand and how your barbers are paid. You also carry the lease, insurance, equipment, inventory and payroll. Your barber license alone does not cover this: New York requires a separate Barber Shop license to operate a business.

The switch point

When does booth rent pay more than commission?

Booth rent starts to pay when the shop's share of your commission earnings on a typical week is more than the rent plus everything you would now buy yourself.

Track your real weekly service revenue for a couple of months, using a typical week rather than your best one, and work out what the shop keeps under your split. Then compare that with the rent at shops you could realistically move to, and add your new costs: products, tools, booking software and card processing fees. If the shop's cut is consistently bigger, renting is probably worth it.

Plan for slow weeks too. On commission they mean a smaller check; on booth rent the rent comes out of savings. Build a cushion that covers several weeks of rent with no income before you switch.

"Booth rent rewards a steady book, not a lucky Saturday."

Taxes, in general terms

How do taxes change when you rent a booth?

This is general information, not tax advice. Talk to a tax professional before you choose a setup and again before your first tax season.

Employee-style

Commission and hourly

If the shop treats you as an employee, it generally withholds taxes and you receive a W-2. Some shops pay commission barbers as independent contractors instead, which moves the tax work to you. Ask which it will be before you start.

Self-employed

Booth rent

Booth renters are usually self-employed. Nobody withholds for you, you may owe self-employment tax on top of income tax, and you may need to make estimated payments during the year. Business costs like rent, tools and products may be deductible. The IRS Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center explains the basics.

Business owner

Ownership

Owning a shop adds business registration, payroll, sales tax on retail and insurance. This is where an accountant stops being optional.

The paperwork

Which New York licenses apply to each setup?

Every model starts with the New York State barber license. Commission and hourly barbers need only that. Owners need more.

The Department of State is clear that a barber license does not let you operate a business: a separate Barber Shop license is needed for that. New York also issues an Area Renter license. Check the official NY barber licensing FAQ before you rent space or sign a lease.

Your first chair

What should you ask before taking a chair?

A common path is commission first, booth rent once clients book you by name, then ownership for those who want it. Whatever the model, a good shop will answer these questions clearly.

  1. How am I paid, and how often? Get the split, rent or hourly rate in writing, plus whether you are an employee or a contractor.
  2. Who supplies what? Products, towels, disinfectant, booking software, card processing.
  3. Who sets prices and hours? Can you raise your prices as your skill grows? Do you choose your days?
  4. Where do new clients come from? A new barber needs a shop with walk-in traffic or online booking.
  5. Who owns the client list? If you leave, can you take your book with you?

Straight answers

Booth rent vs. commission FAQ

What is a typical commission split for barbers?

Splits vary by shop, but commission barbers often keep somewhere in the 50–60% range of each service, with the shop keeping the rest for the chair, products and traffic. Get the exact terms in writing.

Should a new barber start with booth rent?

Usually not. Without a steady client base, rent can eat most of a slow week's earnings. Most new barbers do better on commission or hourly plus commission, then move to booth rent once clients book them by name.

Do booth-rent barbers pay their own taxes?

Generally, yes. Booth renters are usually self-employed, which typically means no withholding, possible self-employment tax and estimated payments during the year. Business expenses may be deductible. Work with a tax professional from the start.

Can I open my own barbershop with just a barber license?

No. According to the New York Department of State, a barber license does not allow you to operate a business. A separate Barber Shop license is needed. Check the official barber licensing FAQ for current requirements before you open.

When should I switch from commission to booth rent?

When the shop's share of your typical week is consistently more than the rent plus the supplies you would cover yourself, and you have savings to carry you through several slow weeks.
