Going to Barber School While Working Full-Time

You don't have to quit your job to become a barber in New York. The state requires 500 training hours for the Master Barber license, and that number is the same no matter how you schedule it. At American Barber Institute you can take those hours in the morning, in the afternoon, or on weekends, pay through a small down payment and weekly payments, and keep a paycheck coming in while you train. It takes planning and some discipline, and plenty of career-changers train this way. Here's how to do it without burning out.

The real trade-off: time, not quality

Working while you train doesn't get you a lesser license. Every schedule leads to the exact same New York Master Barber license, and every schedule covers the same 500 hours of theory, mannequin work and supervised floor time with real clients.

What changes is the pace. A full-time track finishes faster; a weekend track takes longer but leaves your weekdays free for the job that pays your rent. Neither is the "right" answer. The right schedule is the one you can actually show up for, week after week, until your hours are done.

"Employers care that you're licensed and skilled, not which schedule you used to get there."

So before you think about clippers or exam prep, look honestly at your week. When are you free? When are you tired? Who depends on you, and when? The answers will point you to the right track.

Step 1

Choose the schedule that fits your job

ABI runs three tracks for the same 500-hour program. Here's how each one lines up against a typical work week.

If you work a classic 9-to-5, the weekend track is usually the cleanest fit: your weekday job stays untouched and your training happens on Saturday and Sunday. If you work part-time, nights, or a shift schedule, one of the weekday tracks can get you licensed in about four months instead. Plenty of people working overnight, early-morning delivery, restaurant or retail shifts find that one half of the weekday is genuinely free.

New classes start the first Monday of every month (the Tuesday after, when that Monday is a federal holiday), so you're rarely more than a few weeks from a start date. The schedules page lists every start date and the holiday closures worth building into your timeline.

Step 2

Budget with weekly payments, not a lump sum

One of the biggest advantages of keeping your job is that tuition can come out of income you're already earning. At ABI, nobody is expected to pay the full cost before day one.

How it works

Down payment, then weekly

Every plan starts with a $200–$500 down payment at registration (it includes a $100 non-refundable registration fee), then weekly payments while you attend. Your tuition tracks with your training instead of coming before it.

The plans

Three prices, one license

Plan B (afternoon) is $3,600, Plan C (weekend) is $4,600, and Plan A (morning) is $5,600 with a professional tool kit included. Confirm your exact schedule and payment amount with admissions before you start.

Lower the cost

Funding you may qualify for

Veterans may be able to use GI Bill® benefits, and eligible New Yorkers may qualify for ACCES-VR vocational funding. Both can be combined with a weekly plan to cover any gap.

A simple way to plan: once admissions gives you your weekly amount, treat it like a fixed bill. Set it aside the day you get paid, the same way you would rent or a phone bill, so it never competes with everything else. Remember that state exam and license fees are paid to New York, not the school, so leave room for those near the end. Our full cost guide and the tuition & funding page lay out every line.

Step 3

Protect your energy, not just your calendar

Fitting classes into your week is the easy part. Showing up sharp after a shift is the hard part. Barbering is a hands-on skill, and tired hands make sloppy lines.

Sleep

Guard your rest first

When something has to give, don't let it be sleep. Decide your bedtime around your class schedule and treat it as non-negotiable. A rested student learns more in one session than an exhausted one does in three.

Prep

Pack the night before

Lay out your kit, your clothes and your food the night before class. If you're heading straight from work, keep a bag ready so you're never scrambling. Fewer decisions on a long day means more focus for the chair.

Commute

Use the train ride

New York commutes are long. Use that time for theory: review sanitation steps, anatomy terms or New York barbering rules on your phone. Short, repeated sessions stick better than late-night cramming.

Support

Tell the people at home

Let family or roommates know your class days and what you need from them for a few months. Childcare, shared chores and a quiet hour to practice are easier to arrange when everyone knows the plan in advance.

Step 4

Should you tell your employer?

You don't owe anyone an explanation, but in most jobs a short, honest conversation makes the next few months smoother. The key is to present it as a schedule you'll protect, not a problem they'll have to solve.

Go in knowing exactly which hours you need free. If you've picked the weekend track and already work weekdays, there may be nothing to change at all beyond asking not to be scheduled on Saturdays and Sundays. If you're on a weekday track, ask for a fixed shift that sits on the other side of class time, rather than a rotating schedule that changes every week. Offer to stay flexible on the hours you do have, and follow through. Reliability is what earns you the benefit of the doubt.

If your schedule can't bend, that's useful information too. It might mean the weekend track is the better choice for now, or that you start in a month that gives you time to line up a different shift. With a new class every month, waiting a few weeks for the right setup is usually smarter than forcing a start that won't hold.

"Pick the schedule you can keep. Consistency over the full 500 hours matters more than how fast you finish."

Step 5

Practice between classes, even in small doses

Weekend and part-day students have more days between sessions, which means more time for skills to fade. A little practice in between keeps your hands from starting over every class.

Work on a mannequin head at home. You build muscle memory on mannequins before moving to the supervised floor anyway, so keeping one at home lets you rehearse sectioning, guard changes and clipper angles in 20 or 30 minutes on a weeknight. Focus on one thing per session, like blending a single guard line cleanly, rather than trying to do a whole cut.

Drill your sanitation routine. Setting up your station, disinfecting implements and handling your kit in the right order is a habit you can practice anywhere. It's also where many state board candidates lose points, so making it automatic early pays off later.

Keep notes after every class. Spend five minutes writing what your instructor corrected and what you want to try next time. When you only train on certain days, those notes are how you pick up exactly where you left off.

Watch with a purpose. When you get your own haircut or walk past a busy shop, pay attention to how barbers handle the consultation, the setup and the finish. Observation won't replace reps, but it gives your practice direction. If you're still putting your kit together, our beginner barber tool kit guide covers what you'll actually use.

Step 6

Stay on track for the state board exam

Finishing your hours is only half the goal. To be licensed in New York you need to pass two separate tests: a written theory exam and a hands-on practical exam, administered under the NY Department of State, Division of Licensing Services.

Working students have one advantage here: you're already used to managing your time. Use it. Study theory in short daily blocks rather than cramming, rotate through infection control, anatomy, implements, products and New York law, and practice your exam haircut against the clock until the timing feels routine. File your exam paperwork as soon as you're eligible so a scheduling backlog doesn't stall you. If you can, plan to keep some flexibility at work in the weeks around your exam date. Straight answers

Working while in barber school: FAQ

The questions working adults ask most before they enroll.

Can I go to barber school while working a full-time job?

Yes. ABI offers morning (Mon–Fri, 8 AM–2 PM), afternoon (Mon–Fri, 2 PM–8 PM) and weekend tracks. If you work a standard weekday job, the weekend track (Plan C) lets you keep it while you complete the same 500 hours.

How long does barber school take on a weekend schedule?

The weekend track takes roughly 6–7 months to complete the 500 hours New York requires. The full-time morning and afternoon tracks take about 4 months. Federal holiday closures can add a little time, so build in a small buffer.

Do I have to pay tuition all at once?

No. Every plan starts with a $200–$500 down payment at registration, then weekly payments while you train. Plan B (afternoon) is $3,600, Plan C (weekend) is $4,600 and Plan A (morning) is $5,600 with a professional tool kit included.

Is the license different if I train on weekends?

No. All three tracks cover the same 500-hour program and lead to the same New York Master Barber license. The only difference is how quickly you finish.

When can I start?

New classes begin the first Monday of every month, or the Tuesday after when that Monday falls on a federal holiday. The schedules page lists exact start dates so you can line up your work schedule ahead of time.

How do I practice if I only have class on certain days?

Keep a mannequin head at home for short, focused sessions, drill your sanitation and setup routine until it's automatic, and write quick notes after each class on what your instructor corrected. Small, regular practice keeps your skills from slipping between sessions.
